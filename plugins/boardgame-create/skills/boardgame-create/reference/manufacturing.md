# Manufacturing rules for diode-laser board games

Everything the owner has said about how parts are actually made, generalised. The parts generator must follow these; if a rule changes, change it here and in the generator.

## Machine and stock (the author's shop, as the worked example: every game asks for its own at the brief, standard K4)

- xTool S1, 40 W diode laser. Bed 498 × 319 mm, no pass-through. Sheets: 12 × 18 in (305 × 457 mm) plywood, nominally 3 mm (1/8 in); also 1.5 mm. Basswood in both thicknesses, walnut in 3 mm only.
- **Measure the stock.** The owner's nominal 3 mm ply measured 2.67–2.92 mm with calipers (2026-09-15): a 0.25 mm spread within one stock. Ask for caliper readings per stock at the brief (two per stock, the thinnest and thickest sheet: `t3lo`, `t3hi`), put them in `design.md`, and derive every dimension from them: slots and holes from the thickest reading, tab depths and anything that must reach through from the thinnest, so every sheet in the batch fits (every lasergeom fit takes `t`; the page regenerates from the thicknesses the buyer types), with a small clearance. Where parts from different sheets mate (a tab from one sheet in a slot of another), the spread is larger than any sensible clearance, so those joints need a compliant feature or a positive capture rather than a fixed slot width. Box joints may be glued. The owner's chosen answer is thickness-driven geometry generated in the page itself (see `page.md`), so a buyer types their caliper readings and downloads files cut for their sheets.
- Any material the diode can cut or engrave is fair game: 3 mm birch/basswood ply, walnut ply, opaque acrylic, leather. **Never clear acrylic or PVC.**
- The whole game, box included, is made on the laser. Dice would be the only bought accessory; prefer designs with none (randomness at the input).
- **The engraver produces two tones only: engraved or not.** No greys, no white fills, no opacity, no "light" text on bare wood. A second tone needs a second wood (walnut): cut dark characters from walnut with through-cut eyes and scored lines, and light ones from basswood with engraved features.
- Plywood thickness varies (±0.1 mm or more between sheets). The owner (2026-09-15): "press-fit is ok with me as long as it doesn't loosen over time." Rule of thumb: a joint assembled once (standee tab in base, box corners, floor tabs) may be a plain press fit; a joint taken apart every game (a disc carried in a notch, a lid, a token in a pocket) needs a compliant feature (a bump on a flexure beam with a relief slot behind it) or a positive capture (cross-lap, ledge, tab in slot), so it does not loosen with use.

## Cut conventions in the SVGs (kerf compensated in the file, 2026-09-15)

The owner's rule: "we need to implement our own kerf comp because we're mashing the parts together and merging cuts." So **machine kerf compensation is OFF for every layer**, and the files carry the compensation:

- **Kerf is a measured parameter per stock**, like thickness: basswood, walnut and 1.5 mm stock cut with different kerfs, so each sheet carries its own (`lay.sheet(..., {kerf})`; `parts.json` has `meta.kerf` per sheet and `meta.part_kerf` per part). Cut a coupon on sheet 0 for each stock (a 20 mm square and a 20 mm hole, drawn compensated for the current kerf with that value engraved on them), measure both, and enter `kerf = drawn-for kerf + (20 − square)/2 + (hole − 20)/2`; the drawn-for term matters, because a formula without it tells the owner to enter 0 and every press fit fails. 0.15 mm is only the starting guess for 3 mm ply on a 40 W diode.
- **Outlines are offset outward by kerf/2 and holes inward by kerf/2**, so every part comes out at finished size. The geometry in `parts.json` is therefore *file* geometry (oversize by the kerf); `meta.kerf_comp: "file"` and `meta.part_kerf` say so, and the fit checker erodes each part by half its own kerf back to finished size before sampling.
- **Parts that share a cut sit exactly one kerf apart with a single cut on the midline**, so both come out nominal. This replaces "touch and cut on the line"; the old two-colour split (red with compensation on, dark red with it off) and its legend are gone. Red `#ff0000` = cut; blue `#0000ff` = score; black `#000000` fill = engrave (evenodd compound paths make bare holes inside engraved fields); yellow `#ffff00` = the four registration corner marks on backs files, on their own layer so they are never confused with score lines or engraving (owner, 2026-09-15: "make the corner marks a unique color"; magenta was tried first, but "in XCS, the corner markings are still showing up as blue, same as all the other scoring. i want them to be yellow"; a stroke by default, a fill with `corner_marks: 'engrave'`).
- **Files are written landscape with the registration features on the left** ("the svgs should be rotated so that the registration features are on the left"): each sheet SVG is `sheet_h` wide and `sheet_w` tall, with the portrait layout inside a quarter-turn group, so the datum edge and clamp strip are on the left as the sheet lies on the bed. Layout and `parts.json` coordinates stay portrait.
- **Engraving is compensated too** ("shrink the engraved region by the kerf compensation"): union each face's engraving, then shrink it by kerf/2. The minimum stroke (0.45 mm) and gap (0.6 mm) are checked *after* the shrink, so lettering and art are set thick enough to survive it.
- **Edge bleed:** engraving that is meant to reach a cut edge is extended past the cut *before* the shrink: by kerf/2 on fronts, and by 0.25 mm + kerf/2 on backs, because the flip registration is not perfect ("extend beyond the cut by maybe .25mm").
- No live `<text>`: outline every letter (`lasergeom.text`) so the laser software cannot substitute a font.
- Fits are drawn as finished sizes with explicit clearances (0.1 mm on tab slots, 0.2 mm on sliding fits like a neck in a tray, 0.3–0.5 mm where a part must drop in or tip); the compensation above turns them into file geometry. They are **design values sized by analysis for the whole thickness and kerf window**, never tunables the owner adjusts ("if the kerf and thickness is right, everything should fit"); sheet 0 verifies them with one sample of each joint. Check the mismatch case too: files drawn for the full range, stock cut from one end of it.
- Finger-joint notches and floor tabs are **part of the outline, never separate rectangles**: a separate rectangle is cut twice and wastes time.
- Finger joints on **every** floor edge, long edges included; tabs seat in wall notches. A floor held on two edges only will not work.
- Glue is kept to a minimum but not to zero at the cost of presence: no per-piece glue joints on tokens, no multi-part tokens, and no design "over-optimised for minimum assembly" (the owner wants press-fit standees and small structures on the table). Box joints may be glued.
- Sheet margins: 3 mm from the raw edges (1.5 mm from a datum edge cut by the machine itself). Parts at least 1.5 mm apart unless they share a cut, in which case exactly one kerf apart.
- **Corner keep-clear on two-sided sheets** (owner, 2026-09-15: "a 15x15mm triangular keep-clear at the corners of the sheet after the registration strip is cut off ... useful for clamping"): a right triangle with 15 mm legs at each corner of the sheet that remains after the datum cut, holding nothing but the magenta corner mark. Clamp a warped sheet at those four corners, inside the triangles. lasergeom's `corner_keepout` defaults to 15 on two-sided sheets and `check()` enforces it.

## Engraving

- The laser **rasters along the long axis of the sheet**. A thin strip along the long axis engraves in tens of passes; the same strip along the short axis takes thousands. So every text line and the cover composition run along the long axis (on a portrait 305 × 457 SVG: text rotated −90°), and repeated engravings (token backs, card backs) are grouped into as few raster bands as possible (two rows of six, not one row of twelve; discs in the same columns).
- Engraved slots or pockets in tokens are avoided: laser time and depth control. Mechanical features are cut through instead.
- Strokes around 0.45 mm and bare gaps around 0.6 mm after the kerf shrink are legibility guidance, not a limit the laser imposes ("the laser doesn't really have a minimum that we have to enforce per se"); thin results after the shrink are acceptable. Drop specks under 0.6 mm² and 1.2 mm; engraving either stops 0.6–0.8 mm inside the cut edge (charring merges with the edge) or deliberately bleeds past it (see the cut conventions), never in between.
- **Edge scores** (owner, 2026-09-15: "generate a score line on the (kerf compensated) edge of every engraving, except where there's a cut anyway. scoring the edge of the engravings makes them much sharper and they really pop"): every face's finished engraving (after union, shrink and bleed) gets a `#0000ff` score polyline along its whole boundary, leaving out the stretches on or outside a cut, so bleeds get no score; backs are mirrored with the art. Machine order: engrave, then score at low power, then inner cuts, then outlines. `lasergeom` does this with `edge_scores: true` on the Layout or per part; `meta.edge_score_mm` carries the total length. Sheet 0 carries a coupon engraved with and without the edge score, for tuning the score power.
- Page figures of the art are generated from the real cut files, so what is shown is what will be burnt.

## Vector fill: low-density engraving as concentric lines, a whole-sheet decision (owner, 2026-09-17 and 2026-09-19)

"we should probably replace a lot of our low-density engravings with concentric scoring. for example, if we want to draw a large rectangle outline, say 100mm, the laser has to scan across 100mm of empty space in order to draw a 1mm border on each side ... the laser software automatically groups engravings when it saves time." And: "the laser software will automatically group rasters. so a rectangular outline would be very slow as a raster, but if it is filled with other engravings, they'd all get done at the same time. so, it's a pretty difficult full-sheet optimization. we don't know the software's exact algorithm, so all we can do is the best we can (assume they have a good algorithm and err towards raster)."

So whether an engraved element is rastered or drawn as concentric lines is decided per sheet, from the whole sheet, by `Layout({vector_fill: {...}})` (`plan_vector_fill` in lasergeom; on by default in the engine's parts spec and in BUMBLE's generator):

- **The raster model errs towards raster.** The machine rasters in rows a pitch apart across the sheet's short axis, each row its span along the long axis plus a run-in and run-out (10 mm) at 300 mm/s; the software groups objects into one sweep when that is quicker. Since its algorithm is unknown, the raster time of a sheet is taken as the best any grouping could do: row by row, the objects' spans on that row merged wherever the gap between them is under two run-ins, each run costing its span plus the run-in and run-out (`RasterRows` in lasergeom). That is a lower bound on the machine's time, so a rectangular outline costs every row of its box (the two long sides are on every row), whatever is drawn inside it comes free, and a shape is converted only when the saving is dramatic (owner: "we should generally prefer raster where the savings aren't dramatic"): even the most favourable raster must cost at least twice its lines (`min_gain` 2) and the saving left after that weighting must be at least 10 s (`min_saving`) for the shape as a whole on the sheet. The raster option's cost includes the edge score the element gets when its part scores its edges (owner: "make sure you're including the outline score on the raster time, if it is enabled (it adds time but sharpens edges)"); its lines replace that score.
- **A decision is per shape, and duplicates match.** Every element with the same geometry (area, size and perimeter, whichever part it is in and however it is turned) and every copy of it on the sheet convert together, so duplicate geometry always has one engraving style (owner: "any duplicate geometry should match engraving style"), and the saving that has to be dramatic is the shape's whole saving on the sheet, never each little piece's (owner: "30s is too dramatic. there could be 200 little token pieces with rectangular outlines and it could save an hour by scoring instead of rastering"). A shape decided on an earlier sheet keeps that decision on later ones (fronts and backs agree). On BUMBLE that draws the lid's cover frame, the rules and setup-map frames inside the box, the four comb frames' rules and small bee icons, the sixteen card and aid borders on both faces and the twelve wish backs' two frames as lines, and leaves the rest a raster.
- **The decision.** Every connected engraved element (over 8 mm²) on the sheet whose own rows could pay for the shortest lines it could have is a candidate; its saving is the sheet's row time with it and without it; the best gain converts first and every saving is recomputed after each conversion. A part converted on its first sheet stays converted (backs and fronts agree).
- **The lines** (`concentric_fill`, exported): for each polygon of the element, closed lines at 0, pitch, 2 pitch, ... inside its outer boundary, mitred (a rectangle's lines are rectangles, a corner stays a corner), each clipped where a hole cuts it, until the offset vanishes, plus one line along each hole's edge. One family, measured from the outside, so a rectangular border is concentric rectangles from its outer edge to its inner edge (owner, 2026-09-19: "if it is doing a rectangular outline, it should be making concentric rectangles, not picking a seam location and turning around there"; offsetting from every boundary at once met itself in the middle of a border). Pitch 0.254 mm (100 lines per inch, the owner's raster setting) is narrower than the 0.36 mm burn, so every point is burnt.
- **The file.** The lines go on the yellow `#ffff00` layer as `<g class="vector-fill">`, one path per element, in the "engrave and score" section: run at engraving power with the engraving, before the blue scores; the element's blue edge score is dropped (the outermost line is the edge); the remaining raster is written as one black path per cluster of nearby elements, so the machine can sweep the clusters separately. The page's parts keep the full engraving (the renderer and the figures draw those); only the sheet files carry the fill.
- **The designer can veto it.** A part whose look depends on the raster (a face, a photo-like texture, fine hatching) is flagged `raster: true` in the parts spec (or `raster: geom` for the region of its art that must stay a raster; in a hand-written generator, `lay.add(pid, cut, eng, { vector_fill: false | { raster: geom } })`), and the fill leaves it alone whatever the sheet would save (owner, 2026-09-19: "it should be possible for the game designer to flag a given raster as no-scoring-conversion-allowed"); `bg lasertime` counts what was kept. Raise `min_saving` (game.json's parts spec, `vector_fill` in a hand-written generator) to make the whole game prefer raster more.
- **The search.** The objective is the sheet's estimated time with the lines weighted by min_gain: single conversions best gain first with every saving recomputed after each, then pairs that share rows (two borders one above the other each save little while the other still spans their rows, and free the rows together), then a check that every conversion still pays; a local optimum of the estimate, not a proof, which is what an unknown machine algorithm leaves us.
- **The tools.** `node engine/bin/bg.js lasertime [--all]` prints the model, and per sheet the estimated raster time as drawn and after the conversions, the vector time, and every element decided (`meta.vector_fill` in parts.json holds it): compare the totals with the machine software's own estimate and correct the model's numbers (the `vector_fill` options) when they drift. `node engine/bin/bg.js concentric <in.svg> <out.svg> [--pitch] [--burn] [--kerf] [--scale]` turns any shape drawn outside the generator (a logo, a border, from Inkscape: filled closed paths, not strokes) into the same concentric path and says whether it beats rastering its box.

## Two-sided parts: backs first, kinematic-coupling registration

Parts engraved on both sides (standees, discs, tiles with backs, the lid) are done in two passes: backs first, then flip, then fronts and all cuts.

**Which face goes in which file (owner, 2026-09-15):** "i want to swap the back of the box to the top of sheet 1. this is because i don't want any smoke stains on the outside of the box." The face that lies **down on the honeycomb** during cutting is the one that gets stained, so every face that shows on the outside of the finished box (lid top, base underside, the outside of every wall) goes in the **front** file, face up while the cuts run; inside faces go in the backs file. The first reading of this rule put the outside faces in the backs file and the owner corrected it at once: "this is wrong. the outer engravings need to go on the top of the sheet. you put them on the back of the sheet. they will get ugly." Apply the same logic to any part with a display face. After moving a face, check that the mirroring still leaves the art reading correctly and the joints mating the right way round. Engraving the reverse first would put the front cuts off by the raw sheet's size error, so the machine cuts its own datum:

1. Clamp the sheet along one short edge. Leave at least 15 mm beyond the datum cut for the clamp (the files leave 16 mm).
2. Engrave the backs file. It ends by cutting the datum line at the far short edge, from 6 mm beyond the nominal left edge to 4 mm beyond the nominal right edge (so it is cut for any sheet width), leaving two half-round tongues (r 4 mm) standing on the sheet. Half rounds, not triangles: a circular feature locates to one kerf, a V to √2 kerf.
3. Four L-shaped corner marks, on the orange `#ff8000` layer (yellow until 2026-09-17, when yellow became the vector fill), sit 1 mm outside the corners of the bounding box of all the parts on the front (mirrored onto the backs file), not at the sheet corners ("the corner marks should be the actual bounding box of the parts"; "basically if i can get the corner marks onto the sheet, the parts should cut"). Run that layer at score power, or engrave it with `corner_marks: 'engrave'`. **If all four marks land on the wood, every part will cut;** if one misses, reposition before running the front file.
4. Flip the sheet side over side (about the long axis) and butt the fresh edge's tongues into the matching notches in the clamped offcut. The coupling registers the flipped sheet to the machine, not to the sheet's own edge.
5. Run the fronts file: engraving, scores and cuts, 3 mm from the raw edges and 1.5 mm from the datum edge.

Backs convention in the files: the back art is drawn as seen from the back, readable, placed at `bpos(X) = W − X` (lasergeom `Layout.back_x`). For the renderer, a part's `<pid>-back` is that same art mirrored about the part's own bbox centre (`lasergeom.back_art`).

## Nesting and sheet order

- Parallel cut lines between neighbouring strip parts are shared: the parts sit one kerf apart and one cut runs down the midline (`lasergeom.shared_cuts`); walls, neck strips and floor outlines cut once between them.
- **Sheet 0 is a minimal-material mechanical test sheet**: one of every fit (a tab in its slot, a wall corner with a floor corner, a neck stub, a detent or hinge if any, a fit comb, a registration witness: a hole that must land centred on rings engraved from the back). Cut it first and check every fit before committing full sheets.
- Regenerate and check in one command; the nesting check aborts on overlaps and count mismatches; `svg_lint.js` then `fitcheck.js` before the page is built.
