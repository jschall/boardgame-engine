# Designing so the decisions matter

Written after BUMBLE & BLOOM's first physical playtest (2026-09-17). The game had passed every gate: seat parity within a few points, sane game length, goals met at healthy rates, no illegal moves, five judges grading the design against the hit-game table. At the table the owner found that one bee shuttling back and forth was the whole game: "we don't get what the point of more than one bee is ... the gameplay seems pretty lame and the rules are way too complex to justify that." The engine confirmed it in a minute: a player who ignored the dance, the followers and two of its three bees won 66 % of two-player games against the tuned AI. Every gate had measured something other than whether the rules pay and whether the decisions matter. This procedure adds the gates that would have caught it, in the order they must run. `reference/principles.md` says what a hit feels like; this file says how to build one that actually has the feeling.

## The order of work

1. **Action budget first, components second.** Write one sentence: what a player chooses on a turn and how many times ("plant one flower, then fly one bee"). Every component and every rule must consume or expand that budget in a way the player can feel. A component that does not change what the budget can buy is decoration, however pretty it is on the table. BUMBLE's failure in one line: one flight per turn meant a second and third bee changed nothing.
2. **The throughput ledger.** Before any rule text, compute the dumbest sensible loop's rate: how much of the scoring currency a player earns per turn by doing the obvious thing with the fewest pieces. Then write, for each proposed rule or subsystem, what it does to that rate for a skilled player (raises the ceiling by how much) or to the dumb player (adds what risk). A rule that changes neither number is cut before it is written. Keep the ledger in `design.md`; the numbers are the design.
3. **Interaction on the main path.** Anything a rational player can opt out of will be opted out of unless it is strictly better than the main loop. Put the interaction inside the action the player must take: landing on a rival's flower pays the rival; room on a flower is contested; the wasp punishes parking; supply of a kind runs out. A separate opt-in interaction (a dance, an offer, a trade) has to beat the main loop by a wide margin or it is skipped, and then its rules are pure weight. If a subsystem needs its own eligibility list and payout order to explain, it is a separate opt-in.
4. **One budget, one currency, one clock, one verb.** Every subsystem must map to the same action budget, score in the same currency and run on the same clock. Player-count variants written as rule text ("with two players a follower takes...") are a smell: scale by component counts (deck size, seat positions, supply) instead. The turn must fit on the player aid in plain sentences; if it does not, the design is not finished.
5. **Engine and AI before art.** The rules exist when the engine plays them. Write the AI to value only what a player can see on the table (distance, blossoms, kind, whose marker, what is in the row); if the AI needs deep search to tell two moves apart, so would a person, and the decision is opaque.
6. **The dumb-strategy gate.** For each rule and each component, play a strategy that ignores it against the full AI, in every seat: one piece only, never the interactive action, the nearest target every time, random placement, ignoring the secret goals. Record each strategy's win rate against its fair share (1 / players). A rule that costs nothing to ignore is cut or changed until it costs something; a target: every dumb strategy under 60 % of fair share at two players and under 70 % at four, and the one-piece strategy near zero. This is the test BUMBLE never ran; its shuttle scored 132 % of fair share.
6b. **The breaker.** Adversarial play, in the engine: a fresh-context agent is given the sim and the rules and told to write the strategy that wins by ignoring as much of the game as it can, by looping, or by a script it never departs from, and to run it through the same harness. Its best line joins the dumb-strategy table under the same targets. Theory misses emergent exploits; a builder never finds its own. When one mechanic is used every turn by the winner and everything else is ignored, the mechanic is too strong *or* the alternatives are too weak, and the throughput ledger says which: fix the number that is wrong, never buff a mechanic into an auto-win.
7. **The obvious-strategy gap.** The gap between the greedy obvious play and the AI's best play is the game's depth. Near zero means the decisions do not matter; enormous means they are unreadable. Aim for the obvious player winning 30–60 % of its fair share. Measure it with the same harness.
8. **The one-sentence test.** Sample ten positions from self-play. For each, a fresh agent that sees only the table (no engine, no search) must say which move is best and why, in one sentence using visible facts. If it cannot, or it is wrong more than a third of the time, the decision is not intelligible: simplify the rule or make its consequence visible on the components.
9. **The opponent-blindness test.** Play the AI with the opponents' state hidden from its value function. If its win rate barely drops, the game is a solitaire race and the "interaction" is fake. The drop is the interaction's real weight; report it.
9b. **Live paths that answer each other.** Name the game's two or three archetype strategies (the racer, the builder, the blocker: full-strength AIs that lean one way) and play them against the full AI and against each other. Each must be live (it wins between half and one and a half times its fair share at a mixed table) and each must have an answer (some other archetype beats it head to head at two players, 55 % or better). One archetype above 150 % of fair share at every count is the script the game collapses into; below 50 % it is decoration. Rock-paper-scissors at the level of strategy is the shape to aim for.
9c. **Both feedback loops, and the end.** Report the midpoint leader's win rate (who leads at half the expected rounds, and how often that seat wins) and the last-round swing (how often the winner was not the leader entering the last round). A midpoint leader winning more than three games in four at four players is a runaway: name the brake (diminishing returns, a rising price, congestion, exposure to the others) and tune it. A swing near zero means the last round decides nothing: the end trigger or the last-chance plays are wrong. A game that reaches the engine's turn guard has a loop or a stalemate; the guard is a failure, never a safety.
10. **Rewards where they can be used.** An unlock is worth its remaining uses. Count the uses in self-play (BUMBLE's third bag at well 10 was brought home 0.01 times a game). An unlock with under one expected use is moved earlier or cut, whatever the board art says.
11. **Only then the balance numbers** (seat parity, length, goals), and only by tuning numbers and component counts. A balance fix that adds a sentence of rules must remove one or create a decision, or it is refused.
12. **Paper before wood.** A print-and-play from the part SVGs (paper hexes, card stock bases) and one evening at a table before any sheet is cut. The owner may choose to cut first; say what it will cost to be wrong (BUMBLE's lid inside and player aids are engraved with rules that are now stale).
13. **The judges grade the numbers, not the prose.** A judge is given the ledger, the dumb-strategy table (with the breaker's line), the obvious-strategy gap, the one-sentence results, the blindness drop, the archetype table and the two feedback-loop numbers, and forbidden from grading a mechanism it cannot point to a number for. "Stake in others' turns: the dance" is not evidence; "ignoring the dance costs 22 points of win rate" is.

## Complexity budget

Count the rules as sentences on the player aid. Every sentence must name a decision it creates or a decision it constrains. Three tests for a sentence that wants in: does a rational player ever change a move because of it (if not, cut); can the consequence be seen on the table when it happens (if not, redesign); does it exist to fix a number (if so, fix the number with a count instead). BUMBLE's shipped aid had eleven sentences, of which the dance and follower lines (four sentences, two rulings pages in the book) governed play that a winning player never used.

## What the engine must expose (the sim contract, extended)

- `RULES` with every tunable; `Game` with `.current` and `.over`; `playTurn(g, rnd)` for the full AI, so the engine can give one seat another player without forking the sim.
- `strategies`: named turn functions `(g, rnd) => void` for the current seat, sharing the sim's own move helpers: the dumb players for the gate (`onePiece`, `nearest`, `randomPlacement`, `ignoreGoals`, and one per opt-in subsystem), plus `blind` (the AI with the opponents' state masked) and `obvious` (the greedy play a first-timer makes).
- `archetypes`: the two or three full-strength styles for the live-paths gate (`racer`, `builder`, `blocker`, whatever the game has), the same shape.
- `node engine/bin/bg.js balance` always reports the midpoint leader's win rate and the last-round swing; `--dumb` runs the dumb-strategy gate and prints each strategy's win rate against fair share; `--gap` the obvious-strategy gap; `--blind` the blindness drop; `--paths` the archetype table; `--gates` all four. The gameplay judge receives the printout. ORCHARD (`engine/starter/orchard-sim.js`) is the worked example of every export.

## The worked example: BUMBLE's redesign

Rules v2 (`bumble/analysis/redesign/bumble2.js`, 2026-09-17): every hatched bee flies once per turn; a landing takes one nectar; the dance and the followers are gone. The same components, one sentence shorter on the aid. Under the gate: the one-bee shuttle wins 9 % of two-player games (was 66 %), the nearest-flower player 5 %, random planting 27 %; games run 7 rounds at four players; the third bee is hatched in 90 % of games and used every turn after. The rest of the numbers are in `bumble/iterations.md`.
